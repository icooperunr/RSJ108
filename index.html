How to Write a News Article for Beginners

Assembly time: between 1 hour and ♾️
Awesome rating: 100% awesome

Author and journalist Gerald Posner says to start with a topic you care about, do your research, and frontload the most important information. Include the who, what, where, when, and why in your first paragraph. Interview relevant sources, use accurate quotes and attributions, and fact-check all of your information.

  • Things You'll Need

  • Something to write with
  • An interesting topic
  • Time
  • An editor
  • Ability to handle critisism

  1. Choose a newsworthy topic that you care about.

    The first thing is to have some passion about what you’re writing about,” says Posner. “It has to be a topic that you’re interested in.” Before you start writing, pick something to write about that matters to you. Maybe you’re super into arts and culture—try writing a feature on a local art exhibit. Or, maybe you’re invested in politics—do a write-up on an ongoing election.

  2. Do your research and take notes.

    Posner recommends always doing your research before covering a topic. Start by reading existing coverage on the issue to get a sense of pre-reported information. Then, do a deep dive on the specific details you want your article to cover. Make sure you take notes on important information, so you can find it again when you start writing. Tip: Check the references from other articles, so you know where other writers have gotten their information about your topic.

  3. Find and interview relevant sources.

    ournalist Lana Harris suggests finding sources who can give you a story before anybody else. In other words, find people who can give you the inside scoop on your topic. For instance, if you’re writing a story about something happening at your school, you might interview a teacher, administrator, or student council member. It’s important to build trust with your sources, so be professional and respectful when you interact with them.

  4. Answer the five Ws for your story.

    he five Ws are who, what, where, why, and when (and sometimes how). Once you’ve done your research and spoken with sources, ensure that you have enough information to address the five Ws. If you’re missing answers, you may need to do more research or talk to another source. Here are some additional guidelines for answering the five Ws: Who: Who drives the story? Who is it about? Who does it affect? Who benefits and who doesn’t? What: What happened? What are the consequences? What does it mean? Where: Where does it take place? Where should readers go to learn more? When: When did it happen? When was the last update? When will the effects be felt? Why: Why did this event take place? Why is it important? Why should readers care?

  5. Create an outline using the inverted pyramid structure.

    lace the fundamental information (the five Ws) at the beginning of your article and arrange the remaining details in order from most to least important. “Whatever you want your reader to know, you need to tell them in the first paragraph, so they have a reason to hold on and read the rest of the article,” adds Posner. Here are the pieces of the inverted pyramid: The Lead: The who, what, when, where, why (the most essential information). The Body: More details about what happened, elaboration on the lead. The Tail: Supplementary details, minor background info, related context (the least critical information).

  6. Write a strong lead.

    Posner says the lead should be enticing enough to make your reader want to stay and finish the article.[6] Make sure you cover the most crucial information right away in a compelling enough way that someone will want to learn more. Avoid burying the lead—your most important information should be in the first paragraph.

  7. Add crucial context with a nut graph.

    The nut graph is the statement right after the lead that tells your reader where the story is headed—it explains the main point and context “in a nutshell.” Use your nut graph to keep the reader interested and give them a clear idea of what to expect from your article.

  8. Draft the body of your article.

    Now it’s time to put the rest of the pieces together. Explain the relevant context, facts, and statistics to your reader throughout your piece. Make sure you back up all of the information in your story with hard facts and evidence. Use your inverted pyramid outline to guide you as you write your draft. Posner cautions against overwriting and says to include only as much research and details as you need to make your point and move the story along.

  9. Include quotes and attributions throughout your piece.

    Attributions are phrases that identify where you got your information for the reader. This builds your accountability and lets your readers know where you got your information. Include attributions for quotes, new facts, accusations/allegations, controversial information, anything that’s not common knowledge, and anything sweeping in nature.

  10. Copy edit, fact-check, and revise your article draft.

    Now that you’ve finished writing, it’s time to make sure everything is up to snuff. Thoroughly proofread your piece to make sure it’s free of grammatical or spelling mistakes. Double check that all of your information is factually correct and supported by evidence. Then, revise as much as needed. Tip: Ask someone else to read your article, too. They may catch mistakes you missed or point out information that isn’t clearly supported by evidence.

  11. Deliver your article to your editor.

    Once your piece is free of typos and inaccuracies, submit it to your editor (or teacher, if it’s a school assignment). Your editor may have additional changes for you to make, so review your piece with them and incorporate any new feedback before you publish.
